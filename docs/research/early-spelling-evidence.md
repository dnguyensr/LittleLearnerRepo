# Early Spelling and Word Building — Evidence Review

> **Reviewed:** 2026-08-30  
> **Scope:** A local, tap-first English-language learning application for children from pre-K through 1st grade. This review asks what the Words module should teach before a child is expected to spell conventional words independently.

## Executive finding

The current activity displays a picture and every letter of its answer, then accepts those same letters in order. This is useful visual-copying and keyboard-location practice, but it does not require the child to segment a spoken word or map its sounds to letters. The replacement should make the spoken word primary, represent its phonemes with sound boxes, and let the child build a hidden spelling from letter or grapheme tiles.

The evidence supports a developmental progression rather than difficulty tiers based on character count:

1. Notice and identify sounds in spoken words.
2. Connect a small number of known phonemes to letters.
3. Build familiar two- and three-phoneme words.
4. Build regular CVC words and change one sound or letter at a time.
5. Introduce blends, digraphs, silent-e patterns, and vowel teams explicitly.
6. Teach irregular high-frequency words separately and in small groups.

The strongest causal evidence below applies mainly to kindergarten and early elementary children, not two- or three-year-olds. For younger children, the appropriate entry experience is oral sound play and initial-sound association rather than an exact spelling task.

## Current product baseline

The shipped module has 156 words split into three tiers solely by written length: 51 words of two or three letters, 57 four-letter words, and 48 words of five or more letters. The first tier mixes regular CVC words such as `CAT` and `SUN` with vowel teams, diphthongs, open syllables, and irregular words such as `BEE`, `COW`, `BOY`, `GO`, `ONE`, and `TWO`. Four-letter words similarly mix `BALL`, `CAKE`, `FISH`, `TREE`, `PLAY`, and `LOVE`.

Consequently:

- Character count is standing in for instructional difficulty even though the words require substantially different sound–spelling knowledge.
- Every target letter is visible before the child responds, so successful completion cannot demonstrate encoding from sound.
- The full alphabet keyboard creates a search task that is separate from hearing and representing a phoneme.
- The default response speaks letter names; the phonics setting relies on approximate text-to-speech strings that may add a schwa, such as “buh.”
- Two misses reveal the exact next key, but do not explain the sound–letter relationship.
- Session completion count advances the tier even when every answer was copied or hinted.

## Evidence by developmental stage

### Preschool expectations

The federal [Head Start Early Learning Outcomes Framework literacy progression](https://headstart.gov/school-readiness/article/literacy-preschool) distinguishes preschool awareness from conventional spelling:

- At roughly 36–48 months, children commonly engage in drawing, scribbling, letter-like forms, and supported letter writing.
- At roughly 48–60 months, children increasingly connect speech and print and may use invented spelling based on a salient or beginning sound, such as `B` for “bug,” with support.
- By about 60 months, expected indicators include knowing several letter sounds and attempting independently to write some words using invented spelling.

This is developmental guidance rather than a controlled intervention study, but it is important for product scope: the youngest intended users should not encounter exact CVC spelling as the first or only Words activity.

The [National Early Literacy Panel report](https://lincs.ed.gov/publications/pdf/NELPEarlyBeginnings09.pdf) identifies alphabet knowledge, phonological awareness, print knowledge, and early writing among the preschool foundations associated with later literacy. It supports connecting Words to the existing Letters foundation rather than treating spelling as visual memorization.

### Phonemic awareness linked to print

Recommendation 2 of the [IES *Foundational Skills to Support Reading for Understanding in Kindergarten Through 3rd Grade* practice guide](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/PracticeGuide/wwc_foundationalreading_040717.pdf) has strong evidence. It recommends:

- modeling how spoken words break into individual sounds;
- beginning with familiar two- or three-phoneme words;
- using one Elkonin or sound box for each phoneme;
- moving a token or letter tile into each box while saying the word slowly;
- blending the sounds from left to right afterward;
- using only letter sounds the learner has already encountered; and
- introducing common single-letter consonants and short vowels before blends, digraphs, silent-e words, and vowel teams.

The guide specifically recommends continuous consonants initially because they are easier to elongate and distinguish. It also warns educators to avoid adding a schwa after stop consonants during blending.

### Speech pacing and instructional pauses

The IES guide repeatedly describes saying a word slowly, elongating continuous consonants, blending sound by sound, and giving children ample time to focus on a newly introduced sound–spelling pattern. Those recommendations support clearly separating the whole word, its target phoneme, and the sound–letter explanation rather than playing near-duplicate prompts back to back.

The research reviewed here does **not** establish one universally correct pause duration for a toddler phonics application. An observational study of 38 caregiver–preschooler pairs found that longer appropriately placed pauses during shared storybook reading were associated with better immediate recall; the observed appropriate pauses averaged about 0.39 seconds. The study concerned story comprehension, not phoneme instruction, and cannot establish causality or prescribe this interface’s timing. [Zuk and colleagues, caregiver oral-reading prosody study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12704921/)

Accordingly, the application uses a 450 ms boundary between the whole word, one modeled sound, and the relationship statement. This is a conservative product calibration near the observed pause scale, not an evidence-derived mastery threshold. The utterance itself remains at the normal child-facing rate: explicit boundaries are preferable here to globally stretching every syllable. The timing should be adjusted after observed-child sessions if children anticipate the next part, lose attention, or cannot distinguish the modeled units.

A study of 60 English-speaking preschool nonreaders compared phonemic-segmentation instruction using letters and articulation pictures, letters alone, and controls. Both trained groups outperformed controls on segmentation, spelling, word reading, and nonword repetition. The study supports connecting sound segmentation with print, although it does not establish the ideal digital interface. [ERIC record for Boyer and Ehri’s study](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ933946)

### Word building and instructional sequence

Recommendation 3 of the same [IES practice guide](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/PracticeGuide/wwc_foundationalreading_040717.pdf) also has strong evidence. Eighteen qualifying studies examined teaching children to decode, analyze, write, and recognize words; thirteen reported positive word-reading and/or encoding effects.

The guide recommends:

- starting with familiar, regular CVC words;
- modeling before expecting independent work;
- building words with tiles or sound boxes;
- adding one missing letter before expecting a whole word;
- changing one letter at a time to create related word chains;
- progressing to CCVC, CVCC, silent-e, and other patterns only after simpler patterns are familiar; and
- teaching regular and irregular high-frequency words differently.

For regular words, children should apply sound–spelling knowledge. Irregular high-frequency words may need whole-word instruction, introduced only a few at a time because that approach places greater demands on memory. This directly argues against mixing `SUN`, `ONE`, `COW`, and `BEE` in one introductory tier.

### Invented spelling and feedback

The IES panel advises encouraging kindergarten and 1st-grade children to attempt spellings independently, even when those attempts are not conventionally correct. The attempt gives children practice applying letter–sound knowledge; feedback should return attention to relevant sound–spelling patterns and gradually support conventional spelling.

In a randomized study, 56 kindergarten children with low phoneme awareness received sixteen small-group sessions of guided invented spelling, phoneme segmentation, or storybook reading. All conditions also received letter-knowledge training and used the same word set. Children in the invented-spelling condition subsequently read more words than children in the comparison conditions. The sample was small, at-risk, and adult-guided, so it supports guided attempts but does not prove that unguided app practice produces the same effect. [Sénéchal, Ouellette, Pagan, and Lever](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ959103)

A ten-week invented-writing study with five-year-olds also reported later effects on phoneme awareness, spelling, and reading. It was conducted in Norwegian, whose sound–spelling system is more transparent than English, so interface and curriculum decisions should not assume identical transfer. [Hofslundsengen and colleagues](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4980410/)

### Connecting spelling, pronunciation, and meaning

Ehri describes orthographic mapping as forming letter–sound connections that bond a word’s spelling, pronunciation, and meaning in memory. This is a theoretical synthesis rather than a trial of this application, but it explains why a complete visible answer is insufficient: the activity should require attention to internal sound–letter relationships and then reconnect the completed spelling to the spoken word and picture. [Ehri, *Orthographic Mapping in the Acquisition of Sight Word Reading, Spelling Memory, and Vocabulary Learning*](https://doi.org/10.1080/10888438.2013.819356)

## Evidence-to-product decisions

| Product decision | Evidence status | Rationale |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Begin fresh learners with initial-sound listening | Developmental guidance plus phonemic-awareness evidence | Exact spelling is too advanced as a universal toddler entry point. |
| Hide the completed answer during independent word building | Product inference grounded in encoding and orthographic-mapping principles | A visible answer permits copying without sound analysis. No cited study compares this exact screen treatment. |
| Keep the picture and speak its unambiguous label | Supported use of familiar pictured words; exact presentation is a product inference | The picture anchors meaning while audio disambiguates emojis. |
| Use one box per phoneme | Direct IES recommendation | A digraph such as `SH` occupies one sound box even though it contains two letters. |
| Start with familiar two-/three-phoneme and regular CVC words | Direct IES recommendation | This limits each activity to sound–spelling relations the child can reasonably apply. |
| Use a small tray of plausible letter/grapheme tiles | Product inference | It focuses attention on the taught contrast and keeps targets tappable; the exact tray size is not prescribed by the evidence. |
| Model, then support, then ask independently | Direct IES recommendation | Guided practice precedes independent application. |
| Replace buzzing/resetting with informative sound comparison | Product inference consistent with guided feedback and invented-spelling evidence | The response should teach why a choice differs without treating a developmentally useful attempt as failure. |
| Teach word chains and patterns rather than unrelated length tiers | Direct IES recommendation | Changing one phoneme or letter makes reusable structure visible. |
| Separate irregular high-frequency words | Direct IES recommendation | Whole-word memory and regular sound-based encoding are different instructional demands. |
| Use curated local phoneme audio | Product response to IES pronunciation guidance and measured browser limitations | Browser text-to-speech cannot reliably produce isolated phonemes without distortion or an added schwa. |
| Put a deliberate boundary between word, phoneme, and explanation | Direct IES support for slow modeling and ample focus time; exact 450 ms duration is a documented product inference | Prevents the target sound from being rapidly duplicated while keeping each spoken unit natural. |
| Keep independent handwriting outside the app’s claims | Evidence limitation | Tapping tiles can support sound–print mapping but does not replace handwriting or open-ended composition. |

## Boundaries and unresolved questions

- Most strong evidence comes from teacher-led kindergarten through grade 3 instruction, often delivered in small groups over many weeks. The app should supplement caregiver and classroom interaction, not claim equivalence.
- Preschool evidence primarily concerns children around age four and five. There is no basis here for treating conventional spelling as an expected milestone for a two- or three-year-old.
- English dialects differ in vowel realization and, sometimes, phoneme count. Initial content should use broadly stable examples and never treat a child’s spoken accent as an error.
- Emojis can be ambiguous. Whole-word narration is required, and unclear images should be replaced rather than expecting a child to infer the intended label.
- The cited research does not determine the ideal tile count, mastery threshold, animation, reward schedule, or whether children prefer drag-and-drop over tapping. Those are product decisions requiring observation and usability testing.
- The cited research also does not prescribe a precise pause duration for digital phoneme modeling. The 450 ms value is deliberately configurable and belongs in the observed-child review.
- Speech-off mode can remain visually operable, but a sound–letter activity without sound cannot deliver the same instructional experience. Parent-facing copy should state that limitation plainly.

## Product conclusion

Replace the current visible-answer spelling loop with a guided, sound-led word-building progression. Retain the picture as meaning support, reveal conventional spelling after an attempt, and judge readiness by demonstrated sound–spelling patterns rather than word length or copied completion.
